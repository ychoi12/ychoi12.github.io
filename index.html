I study when people should trust AI systems, and how to design them so that trust matches what the system can actually do.

My projects keep landing on the same problem. A question-answering system answered all 258 questions it had no basis to answer. A sentiment model reproduced its machine-made training labels faithfully, yet agreed with my own careful reading of the same tweets less than half the time. An insurance model was 15 points less accurate for young, low-income women than for its best-served group. In each case the system looked competent, and the gap only appeared when I checked it against people.

I come to this from business analytics, operations, and applied machine learning, with degrees from Carnegie Mellon, the University of Illinois Chicago, and Sacramento State. I want to spend a PhD building and testing ways to make that gap visible to the people who rely on these systems, with a focus on education, where a misplaced belief in an AI tutor or grader can shape what a learner does next.

Research interests

Three questions I want to work on

Trust calibration

How do people decide how much to rely on an AI system?

What signals lead users to over-trust a fluent answer or dismiss a correct one, and which interface cues (confidence, abstention, sources) move their reliance closer to the system's real accuracy.

Explanation

What makes an explanation useful to the person receiving it?

Moving from explanations that describe a model (feature attributions) to ones a person can act on or contest (counterfactuals written in plain language), and testing whether they actually change decisions.

Fairness in use

Who carries the cost when an AI system is wrong?

Auditing systems at the level of intersecting groups rather than averages, and studying how proxy variables and biased labels (cost standing in for need, a tool's label standing in for a person's) pass harm through a model unnoticed.

Why education. Education is the setting I care most about. I have tutored business statistics at Sacramento State and I volunteered teaching Korean elders who did not get to go to school as children. Students and first-time learners are exactly the users least equipped to judge whether an AI tutor is right, and the ones with the most to lose when it is not.

Calibrating learners' trust in generative AI: A narrative review of design interventions for appropriate reliance in education

Which designs help students rely on AI when it is right, question it when it is wrong, and still do the work that is the point of the lesson?

Students' reliance on generative AI is often poorly calibrated. In one randomized study, high school students accepted incorrect ChatGPT advice in about half of trials, and among 432 undergraduates, higher trust went with worse ability to tell correct AI suggestions from incorrect ones. I review four families of interventions: communicating the AI's reliability, prompting learners to think before accepting an answer, changing the cost of checking, and building AI literacy. For each, I ask what it actually changes: trust, the appropriateness of reliance, or learning itself.

What I am arguingFor a learner, appropriate reliance has two reference points: whether the AI is right, and whether this part of the task is the thing the student is supposed to be learning. Most interventions address only the first, and the ones that preserve learning tend to help the students who were already best equipped.

Read the manuscript (PDF). Work in progress, not peer reviewed.

Next question. Do trust-calibration interventions help students with low and high AI literacy equally? Almost no study checks, and that is where I want to start.

A tutor that never says "I don't know": Abstention failure in a retrieval-augmented question-answering system and what it means for learners' trust

If a tutor answers every question the same way, whether or not it has a basis for the answer, what cue is left for a learner to calibrate trust?

This paper builds on my retrieval-augmented question-answering project. Retrieval raised exact match from 0.004 to 0.062, but the system answered all 258 unanswerable questions in a 500-question SQuAD 2.0 sample, and its answers looked the same whether or not retrieval had found the source passage. I connect this to research on how people rely on language models, show why standard accuracy metrics hide the failure, and propose a study testing whether a tutor that can abstain helps students rely on it appropriately.

What I am arguingEducational AI should be evaluated on whether it declines to answer when it has no basis, not only on how often it is right. An answer to every question removes the signal a learner needs to know when to doubt it.

Read the preprint (PDF). Work in progress, not peer reviewed.

Next question. Does a tutor that says "I could not find this in your course materials" improve students' reliance on its correct answers and their skepticism of its wrong ones, and does it affect what they learn?

A question-answering system that never says "I don't know"

Does retrieval make a language model's answers more trustworthy, and can it tell when it has nothing to go on?

I built a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline end to end: a DPR bi-encoder retriever, a FAISS index over about 29,000 chunks of Wikipedia-derived passages, and Flan-T5 as the generator. I evaluated it on 500 held-out SQuAD v2 questions, 258 of which are deliberately unanswerable, using exact match, token F1, ROUGE-L, and Recall@K.

What this taught meA system can improve on every standard metric while still giving users no signal about when to doubt it. Grounding is not the same as knowing when to abstain, and I want to study how abstention and confidence cues change what users believe.

Next steps I proposed: fine-tune the retriever on in-domain queries, add a learned "answerable" classifier on retrieval scores, and give the prompt and decoder an explicit way to refuse. I am now developing this project into a preprint on what abstention failure means for learners.

Checking a machine's labels against a human reader

When a model is trained on labels produced by another tool, whose judgment does it actually learn?

I trained a bidirectional LSTM sentiment classifier on 14,427 human-labeled airline tweets, then tested it zero-shot on 201,162 tweets about ChatGPT and fine-tuned it on 25,000 of them. A TF-IDF logistic regression baseline on the same splits showed the transfer gap came from the data, not the architecture. The ChatGPT labels came from an unnamed NLP tool, so I added a step most pipelines skip: I hand-labeled 150 random tweets myself, blind to the dataset's label.

What this taught meFine-tuning looked like a success (73% accuracy), but what the model learned was the labeling tool's judgment, and that only partly matched a human reading. A small, cheap human check surfaced a problem the metrics could not. I also rewrote my own first framing ("measuring public trust in AI") because the data could not support that claim.

Responsible AI for personalized auto insurance

What would it take for customers to have good reason to trust an AI that prices their insurance?

Over a semester we took random forest models that predict whether a customer files a claim and how much, and worked through four questions in sequence: data consent and sensitivity, explainability, discrimination, and the safety of an LLM customer assistant. We used SHAP for feature attribution and Microsoft's DiCE for counterfactuals, translated into plain-language statements a customer could act on ("if your car were three years newer, your predicted chance of a claim would drop"). We audited outcomes with Fairlearn across gender, income, and age, including their intersections, and mitigated with constrained optimization. Finally we red-teamed a LLaMA 3.1 8B insurance assistant, built with a system prompt and RAG documents, against the LlamaGuard hazard categories and ALERT fairness prompts.

What this taught meSafety and trust pull in two directions. The LLM assistant correctly refused discriminatory and fraudulent requests, but it also blocked ordinary insurance questions as unsafe, and that kind of over-blocking erodes trust and access just as surely. Explanations also need a design choice about who they are for: enough to guide a customer, not enough to game the model.

Decision support for an early intervention provider

How much of a sensitive, people-facing workflow should be automated, and who should check the automation?

The provider matched therapists to referred children using years of disconnected spreadsheets, with therapist availability recorded as free text. With a teammate I cleaned and unified the data (names, zip codes, dates, free-text entries), automated preprocessing so it held up as new data arrived, and built dashboards for referral volume by discipline, caseload growth, weekly therapist availability, and zip-code coverage. We also piloted an offline GPT pipeline that turned free-text availability into structured schedules.

What this taught meThe LLM parser failed in predictable places: recurring schedules and therapists who work in several locations. Rather than hide that, we documented the failure modes and designed a manual-verification fallback. When staff feedback showed they valued clarity over automation, we shifted the deliverable toward dashboards they could read and filter themselves, and limited automation to non-sensitive fields for HIPAA compliance.

When cost is mistaken for need

How can a health system ethically use an AI risk score when the data behind it reflects unequal access to care?

We analyzed the population-health algorithm studied by Obermeyer et al. (Science, 2019), which predicted future healthcare cost as a stand-in for illness and excluded race as an input. We compared it with UnitedHealth's nH Predict case (2023 to 2025), where a tool described as advisory allegedly became determinative in practice, and examined bias, transparency, and accountability across vendors, hospitals, clinicians, and patients.

What this taught meA model can be accurate at the task it was given and still wrong about the thing people think it measures. Clinicians trusted a "risk score" without knowing it predicted spending. Auditing the target label before deployment matters as much as auditing accuracy after.

Harvard CSCI E-89B, NLP · Individual

Fake news detection across five model families

Compared logistic regression, a dense network, LSTM, BiLSTM, and DistilBERT on 44,898 news articles. The BiLSTM reached 98.86% accuracy, but TF-IDF logistic regression was within half a point (98.43%), and the strongest signals were source markers like the "Reuters" dateline and sensational wording. I read that as a caution: near-perfect accuracy here reflects how outlets write, which is not the same as detecting whether a claim is true.
